This post inspired me to share some almost completely unknown Black History.

While I have never bothered with any actual Pyramid Schemes, for a few years in the 1980s, I worked with a Black owned and operated MLM company called Lynell's Skin Care.

Lynell Mattox was a down-home Texas farmgirl who looked and sounded like the Baptist preacher's daughter that she was, and although she became a millionaire in Mary Kay, she was not being let into executive meetings and believed it was due to racism.

So she left Mary Kay, found some chemists to create skin care products intended for People of Color, and AFAIK cloned the Mary Kay business program which we taught our organization how to execute in our weekly Thursday Night meetings at Boston's Harriet Tubman House.

With an MLM-based business, the fundamental difference between a Pyramid Scheme and Real Business comes down to one question:

'Can you make money just moving merchandise, or is it only via recruiting and fees?'

Lynell's cost $40 to join, and you doubled your money on the product from Day 1.  We trained you (for free) on how to do group sales presentations (Home Shows) on a part-time basis with the goal of earning $200 profit every Saturday and Sunday afternoon (which comes out to about $9600 / year, which back then meant your kid goes to private school, or you got a decent car now).

We coached folks on how to dress for success, gave them presentation and public speaking practice at the Thursday and other training meetings, coached them on how to maximize their efficiency by encouraging them to stock only the $40 (retail) Skin Care line, which would comprise 80% of their sales, and stay away from the makeup (except for samples that ladies would play with and generally not buy).

As an MLM company, you were also encouraged to recruit (if you wanted to), and the Company set aside (I think) 33% of its margin to be able to pay 7 levels downline (7%, 7%, 3%, don't remember the rest, but probably have it listed in old docs or the proposal I wrote for her to computerize her operation - this was the EIGHTIES, folks).

I was a second level to Lynell, under my friend Karen who brought the business to Boston a few years before I found out about them, and when they were on the rise.  At Lynell's peak (when they had a convention that brought a few thousand to Hawaii, I saw the photos), I think Karen had 670 people in her organization, and I know she lived off the revenue for quite a few years.  

My organization grew to 42 (mostly sisters from the projects) before things started to falter due to logistical issues. FedEx was still new, most businesses were still being run on paper, and Lynell and her family members running the company were primarily salespeople, not business people.

So Lynell tried to get some college-edumacated Black folks involved in running the business, and they tried to take it from her.

Ultimately, logistical issues (unreliable product delivery) and I'm sure issues that I had no visibility into tanked the company.  Not the product (which folks asked about for years, especially Lynell's Almond Oil) nor the business opportunity (which as you doubled your money from Day One was totally legit).

As this all happened BG (Before Google) and it rarely made mainstream news, so you will only find a handful of mentions of Lynell's online, but a few do exist.

So this is something that not too many people know about, but I have a stack of order forms and training materials to prove it, and Lynell is still living on the West Coast.

BLACK PEOPLE DID THIS.
